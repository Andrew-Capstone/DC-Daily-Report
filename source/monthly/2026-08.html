Data Centre Monthly Overview

Monthly Overview — August 2026

August 1–31, 2026 · North America focus
Month in review: August was the month "bring your own power" stopped being a slogan and became policy — PJM formally asked FERC to curtail data centres that can't supply their own generation, Delaware became the first state to require it by law, Alberta moved to bar new facilities from renewable power entirely, and Saskatchewan and Ontario both built it into new provincial frameworks. Gas kept winning the actual megawatts regardless: Amazon confirmed a 7.65-gigawatt private plant in Pecos, Texas that could become the single largest source of CO2 emissions in the country, even as turbine prices raced toward tripling and nuclear edged from pitch to fleet-scale contract with TerraPower's Hyundai-built Natrium reactors. On the technology side, Nvidia's Rubin platform became the first AI system to run fully liquid-cooled with no fans, Samsung and Micron attacked a memory bottleneck now driving hyperscaler capex higher than chips, and quantum computers began moving physically inside commercial AI data centres. Canada had one of its most consequential months yet — Ontario, Saskatchewan and Alberta all released tightened frameworks, Oakville became the first Ontario municipality to enact a moratorium, and Ottawa anchored a landmark quantum-manufacturing investment in Toronto — while public opposition nationally hit a new high of 75%, with at least 18 US officeholders having already lost elections over data centre fights. Financing kept compounding to record scale, led by Nvidia's $500-billion-plus Wall Street financing platforms, even as the first real cracks appeared: Nvidia quietly cut its OpenAI Ohio backstop by $145 billion and underwriters grew visibly warier of "AI debt indigestion."

Energy

"Bring your own power" becomes the default policy answer — while gas keeps winning the actual megawatts

August was the month grid operators and governments stopped asking data centres to wait in line and started telling them to supply their own power or accept the consequences. PJM formally filed its Interim Resource Adequacy Service plan with FERC on August 19, requiring new large loads to bring their own generation or face first-in-line curtailment starting June 2027; Delaware became the first US state to write a "bring your own clean power" mandate into law on August 26; Alberta's grid operator proposed barring any new load over 75 megawatts from using renewable power at all, effectively forcing gas; and Saskatchewan and Ontario both built self-supply requirements into new provincial frameworks. Underneath the policy shift, gas remained the only technology that could actually deliver megawatts on a useful timeline: Amazon confirmed it is financing a 35-turbine, 7.65-gigawatt plant near Pecos, Texas — permitted to emit up to 33 million tons of CO2 a year, potentially the single largest pollution source in the country — while BloombergNEF found the roughly 99 gas plants now being built for data centres nationwide could lift US power-sector emissions by 20%. Nuclear took its most concrete step yet toward fleet-scale deployment, with TerraPower naming Hyundai Engineering & Construction as EPC contractor for up to eight Natrium reactors feeding Meta's data centres, even as gas turbine prices raced toward tripling on backlogs now stretching five to seven years.

Tech

Compute's physical limits moved to the center of the story — cooling, memory and quantum all broke into the data centre floor

Nvidia's Rubin generation became the first AI infrastructure to run 100% liquid-cooled with no fans anywhere in the system on August 21, cutting water use from roughly 2.6 million gallons per megawatt per year to near zero. Memory displaced chips as the binding cost constraint: Samsung unveiled zHBM, a 3D memory architecture stacked directly atop AI accelerators for roughly eight times the performance of HBM5, Micron committed $10 billion to a decade-long push to fix the bottleneck, and a benchmark DRAM chip hit an all-time-high $42.45 as data centres absorbed an estimated 70% of world memory output — the same memory crunch Amazon and other hyperscalers cited when raising 2026 capex guidance. AMD's Helios rack-scale system entered full production with Microsoft Azure as a buyer, giving hyperscalers their first credible single-vendor alternative to Nvidia's platform, while quantum computing moved from research curiosity to commercial infrastructure: Quantinuum and Oracle agreed to install a 98-qubit Helios quantum computer inside a US Oracle Cloud data centre, and Ottawa anchored a CAD $195 million investment in Xanadu's quantum photonics manufacturing plant in Toronto. Floating and orbital data centres also advanced in parallel, with Samsung Heavy Industries moving 50-megawatt moored units into engineering and Starcloud raising $250 million more for its satellite compute constellation.

Canada

Every level of Canadian government tightened data-centre rules at once — while the pipeline and the backlash both kept growing

August may have been Canada's most consequential policy month of the year: Ontario released its Data Centre Playbook requiring projects to cover the full cost of their own electricity with no subsidies, Saskatchewan unveiled a six-principle framework demanding Canadian ownership and self-supplied power, and Alberta's grid operator proposed effectively barring new large loads from renewable power altogether, pushing the province's buildout further onto gas. Municipalities moved in parallel — Oakville became the first Ontario municipality to enact a data-centre moratorium, Rocky View County, Alberta handed elected councillors veto power over new campuses, and Cumberland became the first Nova Scotia council to move toward a pause — while Alberta's own town-hall tour to sell its AI push was met with jeers in Lacombe, Sturgeon County and twice in Redwater. None of it slowed the underlying pipeline: a CBC explainer counted 159 hyperscale data centres now planned or under construction nationally, Ottawa anchored the largest quantum-manufacturing investment in Canadian history at Xanadu's Toronto plant, and DeepInfra opened the country's first Blackwell-GPU inference cluster in Toronto. A Leger poll found 81% of Canadians still worry data centres will raise their electricity bills, even as opposition to specific projects — from Sherbrooke's 9,000-signature petition to New Brunswick's fight over a 400-megawatt Lorneville campus — kept intensifying.

Finance

Financing kept compounding to record scale — but the first real cracks in AI-debt appetite appeared

The four hyperscalers pushed combined 2026 capital-expenditure guidance to roughly $725 billion by August 19, up 77% from last year's already-record $410 billion, while Nvidia moved to underwrite the buildout directly, signing agreements with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilize more than $500 billion in third-party financing. CoreWeave's revenue backlog rocketed to $129 billion within weeks of its earnings report, and Vantage Data Centers began exploring what would be the largest data-centre IPO ever at a roughly $100 billion valuation, alongside Switch's confidential filing at up to $80 billion. But August also produced the clearest signs yet of investor caution: Nvidia quietly cut its financing backstop for OpenAI's Ohio campus from a reported $250 billion to $105 billion, Bloomberg reported banks steering "AI debt indigestion" toward buy-and-hold pension investors after mega-bonds from Amazon, Nvidia and SpaceX traded below issue price, and Broadcom went to lenders for up to $70 billion in debt — owned by investors, not Anthropic — to fund the chips it leases out. Kimmeridge Energy Management warned that as much as half of all planned US data centres could still face delay or cancellation.

Policy

Opposition hit a new national high — and started costing incumbents their seats

Three separate national surveys converged on the same story this month: the Annenberg Public Policy Center found 61% of Americans now oppose new data centres in their area, up 12 points from 49% in spring; Gallup put opposition at 71% in early August; and a Heatmap News/Embold Research poll of 2,045 voters conducted August 8–13 found 75% would oppose a data centre built near them, up from just 43% support and 42% opposition when the same question was first asked a year earlier. The politics turned concrete: a Data Center Knowledge investigation found at least 18 incumbent US officeholders have lost elections in the past year after taking action on a data centre fight, 80% of major-party gubernatorial candidates have staked out a position ahead of November's midterms, and CNBC's Jim Cramer called the backlash a real threat to "perhaps the greatest investment theme in a generation." Governments kept responding in kind — Pennsylvania's Shapiro reversed course to strip fast-track status from Amazon's Falls Township project, New Jersey passed reporting requirements and ended a $500 million tax credit, and Montana began drafting what would be the first outright statewide ban — even as Wood Mackenzie estimated more than two-thirds of the 1,066 gigawatts of electricity requested for US AI data centres is "phantom" demand that will never materialize.

1

Nvidia's Rubin platform becomes first AI system with 100% liquid cooling, no fans

TechEnergy

Nvidia's Rubin generation of AI infrastructure runs coolant up to 45°C and cools every chip and networking component in a closed loop with zero fans, after moving power modules into an independent "power rack." The design cuts water consumption from roughly 2.6 million gallons per megawatt per year to near zero and removes evaporative cooling towers entirely.

Why it matters: It's the clearest technical answer yet to the water fights raging in Virginia, Georgia and Texas, and the first AI architecture to treat near-zero water use as a design requirement rather than an afterthought.
Nvidia → (Aug 21)
2

Samsung unveils zHBM, a 3D memory architecture stacked directly on AI accelerators

Tech

At the Future of Memory and Storage conference, Samsung showed concept models of zHBM, which vertically stacks high-bandwidth memory directly above an AI accelerator rather than beside it. Samsung says the design points toward roughly eight times the performance of HBM5, more than ten times the memory density, tripled energy efficiency and less than half the thermal resistance.

Why it matters: Memory has overtaken raw chip count as the industry's binding cost constraint this month, and a stacked architecture promising an 8x leap is the most concrete answer yet to the DRAM and HBM shortage driving up hyperscaler capex.
Samsung → (Aug 24)
3

Quantinuum and Oracle to put a 98-qubit quantum computer inside an OCI data centre

Tech

Quantinuum and Oracle announced a multi-year partnership to deploy Quantinuum's 98-qubit Helios trapped-ion quantum computer inside a US-based Oracle Cloud Infrastructure data centre, offered as an OCI service for hybrid quantum-AI workloads. It marks Quantinuum's first on-premises deployment inside a major public cloud provider's AI data centre footprint.

Why it matters: Housing a full quantum computer physically alongside AI compute — rather than accessing it remotely — is the clearest sign yet that quantum and AI infrastructure are converging into a single stack inside the data centre.
Oracle → (Aug 11)
4

AMD's Helios rack-scale AI system enters full production, ships to Microsoft Azure

Tech

AMD's Helios — an integrated rack combining sixth-generation Epyc "Venice" CPUs, 72 Instinct MI455X GPUs, Pensando networking and ROCm software — is now in full production, with AMD confirming Microsoft Azure as a customer. The rack packs 31TB of HBM4 memory with 1.4 petabytes/second of aggregate bandwidth, delivering up to 2.9 FP4 exaflops for inference.

Why it matters: It's hyperscalers' first credible single-vendor alternative to Nvidia's platform at rack scale, a genuine test of whether the AI buildout can support more than one dominant supplier.
Data Center Knowledge → (Aug 20)

Micron commits $10 billion to break AI's memory bottleneck

TechFinance

Micron unveiled a $10 billion, decade-long US research push into advanced memory technologies, memory-compute architectures and packaging, arguing that "data movement, not math, is where the energy goes" in modern AI accelerators. Groundbreaking on the associated research facilities is expected in 2027.

Why it matters: The largest dedicated memory-research commitment of the year confirms that the industry now sees memory, not GPU count, as its next multi-year infrastructure fight.
Data Center Knowledge → (Aug 22)
6

TerraPower taps Hyundai E&C to build up to eight Natrium small modular reactors

EnergyTech

TerraPower signed agreements naming Hyundai Engineering & Construction as EPC contractor for up to eight of its 345-megawatt Natrium reactors, with completion, price and performance guarantees meant to make the reactors financeable through conventional commercial debt. The reactors will be built in the US and will supply power to Meta's data centres.

Why it matters: Repeatable, guaranteed-price construction contracts are exactly what advanced nuclear needs to move from one-off demonstration projects to a fleet that can keep pace with AI-driven power demand.
TerraPower → (Aug 24)
7

Amazon discloses ownership of GW Ranch, a 7.65-gigawatt Texas gas-power complex

EnergyConstruction

Amazon has taken ownership of an 8,000-acre site near Pecos, Texas, where permits show a 35-turbine, 7.65-gigawatt behind-the-meter gas plant paired with 1.8GW of battery storage and 750MW of solar to power a new data centre campus. Regulatory filings show the gas plant alone would be permitted to emit 33 million tons of CO2 a year — more than any other power plant in the country.

Why it matters: If fully utilized it would become the single largest source of carbon emissions in the United States, a stark illustration of how far behind-the-meter gas has become the default answer to AI's power needs.

8

PJM's new deal for data centres: bring your own power, or face first-in-line cuts

EnergyPolicy

PJM Interconnection filed a proposal with FERC that would let large new data centres connect and operate before enough new generation is built, in exchange for accepting earlier curtailment during grid stress unless they secure their own qualifying power source. The policy, set to take effect June 1, 2027, is meant to address roughly 70 gigawatts of expected new large-load demand by 2038.

Why it matters: It's the most consequential single grid-policy filing of the year, and the template other US grid operators and Canadian provinces spent the month echoing.

NuScale's 6GW TVA small modular reactor program awaits make-or-break PPA

Energy

NuScale's financing partner ENTRA1 Energy is working toward a power purchase agreement by year-end 2026 for its landmark 6-gigawatt small modular reactor deployment program with the Tennessee Valley Authority — potentially the largest SMR buildout in the world. A signed PPA would let construction begin.

Why it matters: A signed agreement would give the SMR industry its clearest proof yet that reactors can be commercially financed at fleet scale after a string of earlier project failures.
The Motley Fool → (Aug 28)
10

Delaware becomes first state to require data centres to "bring their own" clean power

PolicyEnergy

Governor Matt Meyer signed legislation making Delaware the first state requiring hyperscale data centres to generate or procure their own clean energy — wind, solar or nuclear — over a 10-year phase-in. A facility unable to arrange its own power cannot operate in the state, and the law bars data centre costs from being passed on to ordinary ratepayers.

Why it matters: It's the first state law to combine a hard bring-your-own-power mandate with a clean-energy requirement, a template likely to be tested elsewhere as more states legislate on data centres.
CleanTechnica → (Aug 26)

Georgia regulators approve 3.2GW power deal for OpenAI's Project Camellia

EnergyPolicy

The Georgia Public Service Commission approved Georgia Power's agreement to supply up to 3.2 gigawatts to OpenAI's planned campus in Effingham County, with power delivered in phases from 2028 through 2032. OpenAI, which could spend more than $30 billion on the project, must fund all infrastructure built specifically to serve it and has committed to making up to 1,000 megawatts of demand curtailable.

Why it matters: It's one of the largest single-site power approvals of the year, and a real-world test of whether "curtailable demand" commitments can actually protect ratepayers as promised.

Canada: Ottawa invests $195M in Xanadu's landmark quantum photonics manufacturing plant

CanadaTech

The federal government signed a CAD $195 million agreement with Xanadu Quantum Technologies, anchoring a CAD $893 million project to build "Inception," a 158,000-square-foot advanced photonics manufacturing facility in Toronto's Etobicoke neighbourhood. The plant will handle chip packaging and testing for utility-scale photonic quantum computers and is expected to create 275 high-skilled engineering jobs.

Why it matters: It's the largest quantum-manufacturing investment in Canadian history, positioning Canada as a manufacturing hub for hardware feeding the same quantum-in-the-data-centre trend reshaping the industry this month.

Canada: Saskatchewan requires new data centres to be Canadian-owned and bring their own power

CanadaEnergyPolicy

Saskatchewan unveiled a new data centre framework requiring all future projects to be Canadian-owned and to generate their own power rather than draw on SaskPower's grid; a previously approved Bell project will be the last allowed to use utility power. More than 30 new data centre applications are already in the queue to be assessed under the new rules.

Why it matters: It's one of the most restrictive province-level frameworks in Canada yet, combining a Canadian-ownership test with a hard bring-your-own-power requirement in a single policy.

Canada: Ontario unveils Data Centre Playbook requiring full electricity cost coverage

CanadaPolicyEnergy

Premier Doug Ford released Ontario's "Data Centre Playbook," which will only approve projects that cover the full cost of their electricity — including a new, higher rate class than existing large-industrial programs — and prioritizes closed-loop cooling systems using minimal water. Ford said the province will not offer financial incentives to attract data centre investment.

Why it matters: It's Canada's most detailed provincial data-centre framework yet, though local councillors in Hamilton and Burlington say it still leaves key regulatory questions unanswered.

Canada: Alberta moves to effectively bar new data centres from using renewable power

CanadaEnergyPolicy

Alberta's electric system operator is proposing a rule requiring any new large load over 75 megawatts, including data centres, to be fully self-sufficient for power — a bar that, under current technology and pricing, only gas-fired generation can clear economically. Industry estimates suggest the added demand could triple residential power rates.

Why it matters: It locks Alberta's AI boom into natural gas just as the rest of the industry is racing toward nuclear and renewables, a striking contrast to how other jurisdictions are steering their buildouts.

Canada: Oakville becomes first Ontario municipality to enact a data centre moratorium

CanadaPolicy

Oakville's town council voted unanimously to enact a one-year pause on new data centres, becoming the first Ontario municipality to do so, and directed staff to study whether local rules need updating for noise, vibration and emissions. Mayor Rob Burton said the move was precautionary rather than a reaction to any specific application.

Why it matters: It broke a months-long standoff in Ontario, where Hamilton had rejected a similar pause weeks earlier, and set a precedent Mississauga, Toronto and Waterloo Region are all now weighing.

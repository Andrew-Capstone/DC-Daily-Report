Google and Blackstone's TPU neocloud officially launches as Crux AI

TechEnergyFinance

Crux AI, the joint venture first unveiled in May pairing Google's Tensor Processing Units with Blackstone-managed capital, officially launched under its new brand with a $5 billion initial commitment from Blackstone funds and a target of deploying its first 500 megawatts of capacity in 2027, scaling toward 2 gigawatts. The company is led by longtime Google infrastructure executive Benjamin Treynor Sloss and has hired Meta's former data-centre engineering head, Alan Duong.

Why it matters: It formalizes Google's strategy of selling TPU capacity to third parties through an independently branded neocloud, giving enterprises a non-Nvidia path to AI compute backed by one of the largest infrastructure investors in the world.

PsiQuantum finalizes $100 million CHIPS Act award for photonic quantum chips

Tech

The U.S. Department of Commerce signed a definitive $100 million CHIPS and Science Act R&D award with PsiQuantum, following a letter of intent from May, to accelerate 300mm-wafer production of barium titanate — a key electro-optic material — plus high-temperature single-photon detectors and ultra-low-loss photonic packaging at its Santa Clara facility. The funding targets manufacturability bottlenecks standing between PsiQuantum and fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Why it matters: It's a direct federal bet on domestic photonic quantum manufacturing, the same underlying optical technology increasingly proposed for next-generation data-centre interconnects and compute.
NIST → (Sept 8)

OpenAI pauses ChatGPT Pro subscriptions as new Astra model strains capacity

Tech

OpenAI temporarily halted new sign-ups for its $200-a-month Pro plan after demand for Astra — the company's newest model, launched September 3 and pitched as the start of the "AGI era" — overwhelmed its systems. A product lead said Pro subscriptions "put the most strain" on infrastructure, and the company said it was "pulling all the levers possible" to add capacity.

Why it matters: A leading AI lab rationing its most expensive consumer tier is a rare, concrete admission that model capability is now outrunning available data-centre compute, not just internal roadmaps.
TechCrunch → (Sept 10)

LITEON takes 25% stake in liquid-cooling specialist DCX for $176 million

TechFinance

Taiwan's LITEON Technology is investing roughly $176 million for a 25% equity stake in the parent of DCX Liquid Cooling Systems, a Warsaw-based maker of coolant distribution units, cold plates and immersion-cooling systems for AI and HPC data centres. The deal pairs DCX's liquid-cooling hardware with LITEON's AI server power-management and 800V DC power-delivery business.

Why it matters: It's another sign that power-delivery and thermal-management suppliers are consolidating around the same 800V, liquid-cooled architecture hyperscalers are standardizing on for next-generation AI racks.
PR Newswire → (Sept 3)

Theseus Infrastructure names industry veteran Krupal Raval as CEO

FinanceTech

Theseus Infrastructure — the data-centre development platform Anthropic launched in August with Macquarie Asset Management and Singapore's GIC to build facilities where Anthropic anchors as tenant — named Krupal Raval as chief executive. Raval previously ran Equinix's xScale hyperscale program and spent a year as chief strategy officer at CyrusOne after five years at Digital Realty.

Why it matters: Anthropic tapping a veteran of three of the industry's biggest developers to lead its dedicated build vehicle signals it intends Theseus to compete for sites and construction capacity at the same scale as established players, not stay a passive financing arm.

BetaKit maps how every Canadian province and territory regulates data centres

CanadaPolicy

A BetaKit analysis sorts Canada's provinces and territories into four camps: those actively courting data-centre investment (Alberta, Ontario, Saskatchewan, New Brunswick), those with no standalone policy (Nova Scotia, PEI, Newfoundland, the territories), those taking a mixed approach (British Columbia, Quebec), and Manitoba, which is actively discouraging development. It comes as Ottawa's new non-binding federal principles attempt to knit these divergent provincial approaches together.

Why it matters: It's the clearest single picture yet of how fragmented Canadian data-centre policy remains even as federal and provincial frameworks multiply, meaning site selection will keep hinging heavily on which province a project lands in.
BetaKit → (Sept 8)
7

Nottingham, NH voters approve 12-month data centre moratorium 1,335–143

Policy

Nottingham, New Hampshire residents voted overwhelmingly for a 12-month moratorium on new data-centre construction, following a now-withdrawn proposal earlier this year that drew heavy local backlash. The pause is meant to give the town time to study land-use, environmental and fiscal impacts and consider zoning changes that could make a ban permanent.

Why it matters: A 90%-plus landslide in a small New Hampshire town underscores how broad and lopsided local opposition has become, even in communities without an active project fight at the ballot.
New Hampshire Public Radio → (Sept 9)
8

State data-centre bills hit a record pace in 2026 statehouses

Policy

The Washington Post reports state legislators introduced 375 data-centre-related bills in 2026 through mid-July, compared with 243 in all of 2025 and just 75 in 2024, as growing public opposition pushes lawmakers from both parties to respond. Moratorium bills have been introduced in 11 states, though most have stalled, shifting the real momentum to municipal-level pauses instead.

Why it matters: The bill count is the clearest quantitative signal yet that data-centre backlash has moved from a local nuisance issue to a mainstream item on state legislative agendas nationwide.
The Washington Post → (Sept 3)

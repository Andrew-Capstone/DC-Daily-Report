Google commits $17B to its first Wyoming data centre, backed by a 702 MW fuel-cell plant with carbon capture

EnergyConstruction

Google will build a campus in Laramie County near Cheyenne, called Project Tembo, on 716 acres, with up to 2.7 GW of power and full completion targeted for 2031. The campus is tied to a 702 MW gas fuel-cell development with carbon capture, with partners including Tallgrass, Black Hills, AEP and Related Digital. Google says it will pay the full cost of the power it uses and expects hundreds of millions of dollars in local property taxes.

Why it matters: It is one of the largest single gas-plus-capture power commitments tied to a hyperscale campus, and the 2031 completion date is from a single source, so treat the timeline as provisional.
Source → (Google blog, Nov 5; figures also reported by Baxtel and Letsdatascience)
2

Lambda to anchor Project Red Clay, a 1 GW, $33.1B campus in Lowndes County, Alabama

ConstructionEnergyPolicy

Lambda announced on Nov 4 that it will anchor the Red Clay Technology Center, a campus developed by Cloverleaf Infrastructure in rural Lowndes County. The campus is planned for 1 GW, with room to reach 1.5 GW, and a closed-loop cooling system. The county commission granted a $1.15B tax abatement on a 3-2 vote, and the Alabama Power supply contract still needs regulatory approval.

Why it matters: It shows how far AI capacity is moving into rural Southern counties, and the lawsuit over the approvals means the project's timing is not secure.
Source → (DCD, Nov 4 announcement)
3

Blue Energy and GE Vernova Hitachi advance a 2.5 GW gas-plus-nuclear plant in Texas to power a Crusoe campus

EnergyTech

The companies agreed to advance engineering, licensing and safety work on a 2.5 GW plant at Victoria, Texas, that combines GE Vernova 7HA.02 gas turbines with GVH BWRX-300 small modular reactors, subject to a final investment decision in 2027. Blue Energy plans about 1 GW from two gas turbines for a nearby Crusoe campus in 2030, then up to 1.5 GW from as many as five SMRs from 2032. The DCD headline says 15 GW, but the article body gives 1.5 GW for the data centre.

Why it matters: It is a concrete template for pairing fast gas power with nuclear for data centres, which matters because most nuclear deals for data centres are still years from delivery.
Source → (DCD; agreement reported in 2026, not a Nov 20 news item)
4

Nvidia's AI boom carries $118B in financial guarantees, most of it for one Ohio campus

FinanceTech

In its Q3 fiscal 2027 results, Nvidia reported $115.8B in revenue, up 103% year over year, and forecast $130B for Q4. Its maximum gross guarantee exposure is $118B, including about $105B of credit support for SB Energy's PORTS-Pike campus in Ohio, which covers roughly 4.25 GW across nine buildings that will host Nvidia systems under 20-year leases to OpenAI. Supply commitments rose to $390B, and Vera Rubin NVL72 systems began volume shipments.

Why it matters: Nvidia is acting as a credit enhancer for powered shells, so the $118B exposure is concentrated in one campus rather than spread across neocloud risk.
Source → (Data Center Knowledge / TechTarget, Nov 18)
5

Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia CEO, with CFO Colette Kress named interim CEO

FinanceTech

Nvidia's founder and CEO resigned on Nov 12 after reports of personal conduct, and CFO Colette Kress will serve as interim CEO. The board is considering internal and external candidates for a permanent successor, and Nvidia's market value is about $4.08 trillion. Analysts quoted in the piece expect little near-term change for IT buyers but see risk over 12 to 24 months, because the roadmap has depended heavily on Huang.

Why it matters: Nvidia supplies most of the AI accelerators in data centres, so a leadership gap is now a planning risk for every operator buying its roadmap.
Source → (Data Center Knowledge / TechTarget, Nov 12)
6

Google adds TPU 8tx and TPU 8i Max, with some chips deployable in customers' own data centres

Tech

Google Cloud announced two new Tensor Processing Units on Nov 9. TPU 8tx is a more secure version of the TPU 8t with hardware-level encryption, paired with custom AMD CPU hosts. TPU 8i Max combines two TPU 8i chips in one package with 384 GB of high-bandwidth memory, and Oracle will offer TPUs to Oracle Cloud Infrastructure customers, with select customers able to deploy TPU 8tx in their own facilities.

Why it matters: Letting selected customers run TPUs in their own data centres is a shift away from cloud-only access for custom AI silicon.
Source → (Data Center Knowledge, Nov 9)
7

AMD launches sixth-generation Epyc SP7 and Helion AI racks as rack demand rises

Tech

AMD's sixth-generation Epyc SP7 processor, with up to 256 cores, is now generally available, and servers built on it are shipping. AMD says early Venice demand is the strongest it has seen for any new Epyc generation, with more than 240 platforms committed. Helion open rack designs, with Liquid, Air and Air Inference versions, are reaching customers through November, with partners including Dell, HPE, Lenovo, Supermicro and Cisco.

Why it matters: Denser CPUs let enterprises run traditional workloads on fewer servers, freeing power and floor space for AI, which is the constraint operators now talk about most.
Source → (Data Center Knowledge, Nov 10)
8

Power quality, not just grid access, is now limiting AI growth, a Fluke survey of 500 US operators finds

TechEnergy

Nearly a third of respondents said power-quality issues have reduced their ability to deploy or run high-density AI workloads. Only 36% said their electrical infrastructure is fully ready for denser AI, and 78% lack continuous real-time visibility across critical electrical systems. Seventy-six percent reported electrical or power-system issues over the past year.

Why it matters: It moves the bottleneck from the utility connection to the facility's own electrical plant, where most operators have little monitoring today.
Source → (Data Center Knowledge, Nov 19; survey by Fluke)
9

A transmission fault in Northern Virginia dropped more than 3 GW of data centre load off the PJM grid

EnergyPolicy

On July 22, a transmission line in Ashburn faulted and went out of service, and more than 3 GW of data centre load dropped off PJM, about 3% of system demand at the time. Dominion says the facilities' own protection systems shifted them to backup generation and that no load was shed. PJM has not released a preliminary analysis, and the cause of the fault has not been disclosed.

Why it matters: It is the second large event of this kind in Northern Virginia after a 1.5 GW drop in 2024, and it came months after NERC's May 2026 alert on computational load, so regulators are watching ride-through behaviour closely.
Source → (DCD; event of July 22, 2026, reported later)

Five ways data centre operators are securing power as grid limits tighten

Energy

The article describes five approaches: fixed-price power purchase agreements, behind-the-meter on-site generation, microgrids that combine sources with storage, phased energisation that starts operations in stages, and front-of-the-meter arrangements that feed generation back to the grid. It names no specific deals or figures and treats the list as a practical guide for large operators.

Why it matters: It is a useful checklist for how power is being contracted now, though it is analysis rather than a new deal.

11

A White House push for onsite power meets the practical limits of AI data centre builds

PolicyEnergy

In an Oct 21 post, President Trump said large new data centres should generate their own electricity and return any surplus to the grid or local community. The article argues most AI sites will need hybrid designs, and it notes that some turbine models are reportedly sold out through 2031. Examples include Stargate Abilene, which uses gas, fuel cells and gas engines during construction, and Applied Digital's Ellendale site, supplied by 400 MW of stranded wind.

Why it matters: A federal preference for self-supply would collide with projects that already hold grid connections or PPAs, so it could reshape many existing plans.
Source → (Data Center Knowledge, Nov 3)
12

Public pushback is turning states that welcomed data centres toward pauses and stricter rules

PolicyConstruction

In Pennsylvania, gubernatorial candidate Stacy Garrity moved from praising Amazon's $20B investment to backing a pause on new development, and Gov. Josh Shapiro has pushed for stricter environmental and transparency rules. In Wisconsin, Gov.-elect David Crowley reversed his opposition to a moratorium and is considering ending the industry's state sales-tax exemption. GlobalData says projects that broke ground between Q3 2025 and Q3 2026 waited about two years from announcement to construction, up from about 18 months earlier.

Why it matters: Losing tax exemptions and adding community conditions raises developers' costs, and the longer approval timelines show the effect is already in the schedules.
Source → (Data Center Knowledge, Nov 18)
13

After the midterms, more cities move to restrict data centres as voters back local limits

Policy

Voters in 10 states approved nearly every local data centre restriction on the Nov 3 ballot. Newsweek reports 46 of 47 measures backed tighter controls by Nov 4, while another tally counts 40 of 41; the counts differ by source. Edmond, Oklahoma extended its moratorium to June 30, 2027, and Boise, Idaho approved a moratorium on "mega data centres" after a Nov 10 hearing.

Why it matters: Local ballot results give opposition a democratic mandate that is harder for developers and state officials to override than a council vote.
Source → (Newsweek, Nov 4–10; counts vary by tracker)

Mississauga approves a one-year freeze on data centres above 10 MW

CanadaPolicy

Mississauga council voted unanimously on Sept 16 for a one-year freeze on data centres with energy demand above 10 MW, roughly the power for 10,000 homes. The pause appears to put a 40 MW Prologis project in a northwest business park in limbo, and the company said it was disappointed and is considering next steps.

Why it matters: It is one of several Ontario municipalities pausing projects while the province and cities disagree on how to regulate them.

Ottawa launches Responsible Data Centre Development Principles, signed by major AI and cloud firms

CanadaPolicy

The federal government set out expectations on electricity costs, water use and local benefits for data centres. Listed signatories include AWS, Anthropic, Bell, Cohere, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI and TELUS. The principles are voluntary and lack binding definitions or penalties.

Why it matters: It sets the national baseline that provinces are now turning into rules, and its voluntary status is the gap critics point to.
Source → (Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada, Sept 3; older than the 24-hour window)
18

Oakville becomes the first Ontario town to enact a data centre moratorium

CanadaPolicy

Oakville council passed a one-year moratorium on new data centre construction unanimously in August, becoming the first municipality in Ontario to do so. Land records show a Hive subsidiary bought property in Oakville days before the announcement. Hamilton, by contrast, rejected a one-year pause on July 15 by a 10-6 vote and is studying guardrails instead.

Why it matters: It shows Ontario's municipalities diverging, with some pausing and others choosing guardrails, which will shape where Canadian capacity is built.
Source → (CTV News, Aug 2026; older than the 24-hour window)

Morning brief: Two hyperscalers dropped blockbuster numbers today: Microsoft disclosed it signed more than $130 billion in new data-centre leases last quarter alone — pushing total future lease commitments to $329.1 billion — while pledging to roughly double its capacity within two years, and Meta lifted the floor on its 2026 AI capex to as much as $145 billion as it races to double compute to 7 gigawatts this year and 14 next. On the chip side, TSMC padded its Arizona buildout with another $100 billion and AMD rolled out its first 2-nanometer EPYC processors built for agentic AI workloads.

Microsoft signs $130 billion in new data-centre leases in a single quarter, commitments hit $329.1 billion

FinanceTech

Microsoft disclosed it signed more than $130 billion in new data-centre leases during its fiscal fourth quarter, pushing total commitments for leases not yet begun to $329.1 billion, up from $196.6 billion the prior quarter. The company brought 31 new data centres online in the quarter (88 over the fiscal year), and CEO Satya Nadella said Microsoft is on pace to roughly double its data-centre capacity over the next two years, with capital expenditures hitting $41 billion for the quarter, up 70% year-over-year.

Why it matters: It's one of the largest single-quarter infrastructure commitments any hyperscaler has disclosed, underscoring how far ahead of actual buildout the AI capacity race has run.

Meta lifts 2026 AI capex floor to $145 billion, plans to double compute to 14 gigawatts by next year

FinanceTech

Meta narrowed its 2026 capital-spending guidance upward to a $130–145 billion range (from $125–145 billion) as its AI buildout consumed nearly all second-quarter operating cash flow, with free cash flow falling to $784 million from $8.55 billion a year earlier. Meta plans to double its overall computing power to 7 gigawatts this year and double it again to 14 gigawatts in 2027.

Why it matters: A hyperscaler's free cash flow nearly disappearing under AI capex, even as it raises spending guidance again, shows how thin the margin for error has become across the industry's biggest balance sheets.

Canada: Ontario says AI data centres must deliver "community benefits" and pay full power costs to connect

CanadaPolicy

Ontario Energy Minister Stephen Lecce said companies seeking to connect large AI data centres to the province's grid will need to demonstrate "community benefits," pay the full cost of the electricity they consume, and satisfy a broader set of provincial priorities — though the government has yet to publish the actual approval criteria. The announcement comes as Ontario's grid operator, the IESO, reports data-centre connection requests have reached roughly 6,000 megawatts, up 70% in just two months.

Why it matters: As the province with the largest share of Canada's active data centres, Ontario's shift toward discretionary approval criteria could become the template — or the roadblock — other provinces measure their own data-centre policies against.

4

Montgomery County, Maryland passes 18-month moratorium on new data-centre permits

PolicyConstruction

The Montgomery County Council unanimously approved an 18-month moratorium blocking the county's Department of Permitting Services from issuing new data-centre permits, alongside a zoning measure narrowing where and how future projects can be built. The move follows County Executive Marc Elrich's earlier six-month executive-order pause and is meant to give the county time to write comprehensive rules governing size, siting and environmental review, including protection of the Potomac River watershed.

Why it matters: It's one of the most aggressive local moratoriums yet imposed in the Washington, D.C. data-centre corridor, adding to a growing list of jurisdictions pausing approvals faster than developers can adapt.

5

Texas approves first co-located AI data centre/wind-farm deal, with a 30-minute curtailment rule

EnergyPolicy

The Public Utility Commission of Texas approved a net-metering arrangement letting Crusoe build a 260-megawatt AI data centre for customer Ensign directly alongside a roughly 265-megawatt wind farm near Hutto, Texas, behind an Oncor substation. The deal requires the facility to curtail its full load within 30 minutes of a grid-emergency notice and bars it from separately participating in paid demand-response programs.

Why it matters: It's the first Texas approval of its kind, and the curtailment terms could set the template ERCOT uses to let dozens of other proposed AI campuses co-locate with renewables without straining the grid.

6

INNIO books 1.1-gigawatt gas-engine order for a major US data-centre campus

Energy

Distributed-power supplier INNIO said it secured an order for roughly 1.1 gigawatts of gas-engine capacity — more than 200 Jenbacher J624 units — to be delivered over several years for an undisclosed major US data-centre developer. The containerized engines are designed for behind-the-meter prime power, offering faster deployment and lower redundancy needs than traditional grid interconnection.

Why it matters: Another gigawatt-scale, single-customer gas order shows how much of the industry's near-term power gap is being filled by containerized, on-site generation rather than new grid capacity.

7

White House expands data-centre ratepayer pledge to 187 utilities as House panel advances companion bill 52-0

Policy

The White House grew its voluntary Ratepayer Protection Pledge — which commits signatories to fund data-centre power infrastructure without passing costs to households — to 187 utilities, cooperatives and developers plus 23 governors, covering roughly 80% of power delivered to US homes and businesses. Separately, the House Energy and Commerce Committee voted 52-0 to advance the bipartisan Ratepayer Protection Act, which would require state regulators to consider making large loads over 100 megawatts cover the full incremental cost of the infrastructure they trigger.

Why it matters: A voluntary pledge backed by a near-unanimous bipartisan bill signals cost-allocation protections are moving from a state-by-state fight toward a national floor.
POWER Magazine →
8

TSMC adds another $100 billion to its Arizona chip-manufacturing buildout

TechFinance

TSMC told investors on its latest earnings call that it is increasing its planned US investment by $100 billion, bringing its total committed spending on Arizona manufacturing facilities to roughly $265 billion. The expansion follows a quarter in which TSMC's revenue grew 34% year-over-year to $40 billion, driven largely by AI chip demand, with record free cash flow of $40 billion over the past twelve months.

Why it matters: It's the clearest sign yet that the chip foundry underpinning nearly every AI data centre's GPUs is scaling US production fast enough to become a genuine alternative to Taiwan-based fabs.

9

AMD launches EPYC 9006, its first data-centre CPU built on TSMC's 2-nanometer process

Tech

AMD unveiled its sixth-generation EPYC 9006 server processors, the company's first high-performance-computing chip manufactured on TSMC's advanced 2-nanometer node, targeting agentic AI, cloud and enterprise workloads alongside traditional HPC. The launch widens AMD's push to make CPUs a bigger part of AI data-centre infrastructure rather than a supporting role behind GPUs.

Why it matters: A more capable CPU tier could shift some AI-adjacent workloads away from scarce, expensive GPU accelerators, easing part of the industry's chip bottleneck.
DigiTimes →
10

Compass Datacenters and a Mississippi community college launch the state's first data-centre workforce program

Construction

Compass Datacenters and Meridian Community College launched the 14-week MEI Data Center Pathway Program, offering free, no-experience-required training in the mechanical, electrical and IT systems used to run data centres, with Compass funding full tuition for the inaugural cohort starting this fall. The program feeds directly into Compass's planned $10 billion data-centre campus in Meridian, with industry partners including Schneider Electric, Siemens and Vertiv already lined up to hire graduates.

Why it matters: With skilled-trade labor now one of the tightest bottlenecks in data-centre construction, purpose-built local training pipelines like this one are becoming as important to a project's timeline as land or power.

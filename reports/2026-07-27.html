1

Nvidia in talks to guarantee $250 billion in financing for OpenAI's 10GW Ohio megasite

TechEnergyFinance

Nvidia is reportedly negotiating to backstop roughly $250 billion of debt and lease financing so OpenAI can occupy a 10-gigawatt data centre that SoftBank's energy arm is developing on a former uranium enrichment site in southern Ohio, with the Wall Street Journal putting the full project's cost — including chips — above $500 billion. The first 800MW phase is targeted for 2028; Nvidia is separately discussing financing up to $350 billion of OpenAI's chip purchases, though terms remain unsettled and Reuters could not independently verify the report.

Why it matters: A guarantee this size would be the largest infrastructure financing commitment ever tied to a single AI company, and would mark OpenAI's first real step toward owning its compute instead of renting it from Microsoft, Amazon and Oracle.

2

Samsung Heavy and Sargent & Lundy to jointly develop universal floating SMR platform

TechEnergy

Samsung Heavy Industries and Chicago-based nuclear engineering firm Sargent & Lundy signed a memorandum of understanding on July 21 to jointly develop a technology-agnostic floating small modular reactor platform capable of accommodating multiple reactor designs. The partnership moves Samsung Heavy's floating SMR programme beyond the conceptual design it completed in 2025 and into licensing, plant standardisation, US regulatory approval and commercial deployment planning.

Why it matters: Offshore, reactor-agnostic nuclear platforms sidestep the land-use and grid-interconnection fights slowing onshore projects, giving data centre developers a potential new pathway to firm, carbon-free power at gigawatt scale.

3

AMD partners with Cerebras on disaggregated AI inference platform to counter Nvidia-Groq

Tech

AMD announced a partnership with Cerebras Systems at its Advancing AI keynote on July 23 to build a disaggregated compute platform pairing Instinct GPUs with Cerebras' SRAM-based accelerators for ultra-low-latency, high-throughput agentic AI inference. The joint system is expected to become available through Cerebras Cloud in the second half of 2026 and directly addresses the speed advantage Nvidia gained by acquiring Groq for roughly $20 billion in December.

Why it matters: Pairing two of Nvidia's biggest inference-side challengers gives hyperscalers a credible alternative for latency-sensitive agentic workloads, a segment increasingly seen as the next major battleground in AI infrastructure.

4

Gigawatt-scale campus planned for Lovejoy, Georgia sidesteps neighbouring county's moratorium

EnergyConstruction

Stillwater Development filed a development-of-regional-impact application for the 15-building Stillwater Technology Park in Lovejoy, a total of up to 3.465 million square feet, with Central Georgia EMC set to provide a 1.25GW grid connection. The site sits just inside Lovejoy's city limits, exempting it from the data-centre moratorium neighbouring Clayton County has placed on unincorporated land.

Why it matters: Developers routing gigawatt-scale projects around a single municipal boundary to dodge a county-wide pause shows how easily local moratoriums can be outmanoeuvred without regional or state-level coordination.

5

Burlington, Ontario council votes down data centre moratorium, orders staff review instead

CanadaPolicy

Burlington city council voted down Ward 2 councillor Lisa Kearns' July 21 motion to study a one-year moratorium on data centre development, with only Mayor Marianne Meed Ward joining her in support. Council did direct staff to review the city's data-centre rules even as it continues weighing a developer's application for a two-storey facility.

Why it matters: Burlington's vote, coming days after Hamilton's own moratorium failed 10-6, suggests Ontario municipalities are converging on "study, don't pause" as their default response, a softer approach than the outright bans spreading in the US.

6

Hundreds march in Vancouver for third major protest against planned Telus AI data centres

CanadaPolicy

The No AI Vancouver group held its third major demonstration on July 25–26, rallying at the Vancouver Art Gallery before marching through downtown streets to oppose Telus's planned AI data centres in Mount Pleasant and at 150 West Georgia Street. Organizers estimated turnout at several hundred, following crowds of 500–1,200 at their May and June demonstrations.

Why it matters: Three consecutive months of growing street protests in Canada's third-largest city shows local opposition hardening rather than fading, even as the projects continue moving through municipal review.

7

Hydro-Québec pushes to roughly double electricity rates for large data centres

CanadaEnergy

Hydro-Québec has asked the province's energy regulator to raise rates for data centres consuming more than 5MW to about 13¢/kWh — roughly double what large-power customers currently pay — phased in over five years for facilities already connected. A hearing before the Régie de l'énergie is expected this fall, with a decision possible by early 2027; some data-centre operators have already signalled they will contest the tariff.

Why it matters: Quebec has marketed itself as a low-cost, hydro-powered destination for data centres, so a utility-led move to double rates would reshape the province's competitive pitch just as demand is projected to grow sevenfold by 2035.

8

Angus Reid poll: 68% of Canadians would oppose a data centre built near their home

CanadaPolicy

An Angus Reid Institute survey of 1,803 adults found 68% would oppose a large data centre within a few blocks of home, rising to 73% among rural respondents and 71% in British Columbia, versus just 19% opposition in Quebec. Two-thirds also said data centres are bad for energy and environmental sustainability, and 52% called them bad for job creation.

Why it matters: A national opposition rate this high, and this uneven across provinces, gives local politicians fresh cover to resist projects even as federal and provincial governments continue courting the investment.

9

Montgomery County, Maryland committees advance 18-month data centre moratorium with 25MW cap

Policy

Two Montgomery County Council committees voted on July 22 to advance an 18-month moratorium on new data centres, codifying and extending County Executive Marc Elrich's June executive order, alongside zoning text amendments that would bar any facility larger than 25 megawatts. The full council is scheduled to hold a final vote this week.

Why it matters: A 25MW cap would rule out virtually every hyperscale-class project, making Montgomery County one of the most restrictive major US jurisdictions yet if the full council follows its committees.

10

Guadalupe County, Texas residents sue over data-centre meeting transparency

PolicyConstruction

An attorney with the Save Our Springs Alliance filed suit against the Guadalupe County Commissioners Court on July 24, alleging a pattern of meetings on the Palomino Alpha and Cloudburst data-centre projects that violated the Texas Open Meetings Act and Tax Code. The suit also flags that County Judge Kyle Kutscher owns more than 100 acres on the Palomino Alpha site, while residents separately cite water, air-quality and transparency concerns.

Why it matters: Procedural lawsuits like this one are becoming a standard tool for communities to slow projects even where zoning fights alone have failed, adding legal risk to the approval timeline for Texas's fast-growing pipeline.

11

Ohio Governor DeWine signs White House Ratepayer Protection Pledge

PolicyEnergy

Ohio Governor Mike DeWine signed President Trump's Ratepayer Protection Pledge on July 24, joining roughly 22 other Republican governors in the non-binding commitment to keep data-centre and AI infrastructure costs off residential utility bills. Ohio hosts about 200 data centres, the sixth-most of any state, and DeWine had already paused new data-centre tax exemption approvals in late May.

Why it matters: With Ohio now home to major projects like SoftBank's proposed Ohio megasite, its governor's ratepayer commitment signals state-level political cover is building even for states courting the largest AI infrastructure investments.

12

Champaign County, Illinois labor unions publicly back data centre construction

ConstructionPolicy

Iron Workers Local 380 business manager Charles Black wrote to the Champaign County Board this month urging support for pending data-centre projects, arguing the temporary construction jobs "create careers, allow people to support families, buy homes, and promote stability." Local electrical workers separately said they remain optimistic developers will use union labour, despite broader concerns about nonunion contractors on other projects nationally.

Why it matters: Organized labour publicly siding with developers, rather than opposition groups, complicates the simple "communities versus data centres" narrative and gives local officials a countervailing constituency to weigh against moratorium pushes.

13

Nearly a third of Indiana counties have now restricted data centre development

Policy

A statewide tally finds 11 Indiana counties have passed data-centre ordinances, 17 have enacted temporary moratoriums, and two — Marshall and Cass — have imposed outright bans on new development, covering roughly a third of the state's 92 counties. Researchers cite water and electricity consumption, land use, noise and uncertain long-term job benefits as the drivers.

Why it matters: Indiana's rapid, county-by-county patchwork of restrictions illustrates how quickly local governments are moving to get ahead of hyperscale projects even without statewide legislation forcing their hand.
